I should tell you upfront: sports and I are not in a healthy relationship.

It has never been. If you had to describe it romantically, and bear with me, because someone once did, it goes something like this. Boy meets sports. Sports is electrifying, intoxicating, all-consuming. Boy gives sports everything he has. Sports, unbothered, dumps boy in the third quarter of a playoff game and leaves him staring at the ceiling at 2AM, wondering what he did wrong. The highs keep boy coming back. The lows restructure his entire personality.

I've been in this thing since I was six. That's going on fifteen years now. And I still don't know if I'm winning.

![0.6](/images/cartwheel.png)

*The cartwheel days.*

But there was one night, and I mean one specific night, not a montage, not a highlight reel, that made all of it make sense.

Dave & Busters, sometime in October 2013. The kind of place that's always too loud and never quite the right temperature, arcade lights doing their best impression of a casino floor. I was eight. My dad had dragged me there on a *weekday*, which in itself was alarming... we were a weekend family, creatures of routine, and my mom hadn't come along. He wanted to watch Game 6 of the World Series. The Red Sox were up. He'd been rooting for them that year with a conviction that felt new, almost like he was making a statement about something larger than baseball. (The Boston Marathon tragedy had happened earlier that year. I was too young to fully understand it. But I understood that my dad was angry about it in the way adults get angry when the world does something they can't explain to their kids.)

By the fourth inning, the Sox were up by six. Every passing inning felt like something precious being slowly handed back. The food on the table went cold. My dad stopped talking.

And then, bottom of the ninth, Koji Uehara, one pitch... Carpenter went down swinging.

I looked at my dad. And I saw something I'd genuinely never seen before: the man was *undone*. Fist pumping, laughing, eyes glassy. I didn't fully understand what had just happened on the field. But I understood what was happening in front of me. I hugged him. And I cried. Not the performed crying of an eight-year-old who wants attention, but the real kind, the kind that catches you by surprise, that comes from somewhere you didn't know existed.

That was the night baseball stopped being something I watched and became something I *felt*. The diamond on a flickering Dave & Busters television screen, that was where it started.

![0.8](/images/koji.jpg)

*The winning moment of the Red Sox*

Getting there had taken years of my dad throwing things at a wall to see what stuck.

Basketball. Tennis. Swimming. Soccer. Karate. Cricket. He enrolled me in all of them, with the cheerful optimism of a man who was absolutely certain his son was going to be exceptional at *something*, we just hadn't found it yet.

The soccer years are perhaps the most humbling chapter of my athletic biography. Five years of practice. I've score just one goal ever. One, scored in a county-level U-11 game, and my parents, who attended every single match and every single practice, managed to miss it. Every other kid on the field scored that day. The universe, it turned out, has a sense of irony.

![swimming](/images/soccer.JPG)

*Exhibit A of the kid with the participation trophy*

Basketball had the same energy. I was reasonably tall in 6th grade, which felt promising until it became clear that "reasonably tall for 6th grade" doesn't translate into any meaningful advantage when the kids you're playing against are already the height of grown men and significantly more athletic. You learn a lot about yourself going up against someone who's physically superior in every measurable way. Most of what you learn is humbling.

![0.7](/images/swimming.JPG)

*Taken after winning a swimming medal*

What eventually stuck, what I turned out to have something of a hand at, was baseball, and then cricket. The latter arrived properly at the end of 7th grade. I was fast. Won the 100m and 200m in 8th and 9th grade, back when my body still cooperated with my ambitions. I bowled with pace. My batting, though, was a different story. Technically present, practically unremarkable. Something was missing. Some belief, maybe. Some willingness to trust the innings would turn if I just stayed at the crease long enough.

That missing thing arrived on the night of May 29, 2023.

CSK vs GT. IPL Final. Four days before JEE Advanced, the exam that determines whether you get into the best engineering institutions in the country, the exam I'd spent two years preparing for. My dad had, reasonably, said: no IPL this season. Too much distraction, too close to the exam. And I'd agreed. But he gave me a pass for this game. The final. Just this once.

What unfolded over those hours was the kind of cricket that makes you understand why people dedicate their lives to this sport. Everything was against Chennai that season. Their bowling attack held together by Deshpande and a lot of hope and faith, Sai Sudarshan putting up a century in the finals for the opposition, the last over a genuine, stomach-clenching choke. And then Jadeja. The last ball. The kind of moment that doesn't feel real while it's happening, only in the hours and days after.

![0.8](/images/jadeja.png)

*Right after the winning moment by Jadeja*

I was running around the house at 3AM. I'm not exaggerating when I say I was *running*... lapping the living room, jumping, making sounds that no 18-year-old preparing for the most important exam of his life should be making at that hour. My dad was watching with the particular quiet joy of a man who has learned, over the years, to contain his excitement, but the containment was barely holding. He was grinning. That grin I knew.

What that match gave me... and I mean this not metaphorically but practically, was a lesson about what happens when you stay. When everything says it's over and you don't accept that conclusion. Four days later, I walked into JEE Advanced carrying something extra. Jadeja had shown me what the long game looks like. You don't need momentum to win. You need one good over at the end.

My batting got better after that. Genuinely better. Not because my technique changed, but because my relationship with pressure did.

I think about what sport has actually cost me, beyond the losses and the embarrassments and the Dave & Busters baseball dinner nights.

It cost me certainty. **Sports doesn't let you be certain about anything. You can do everything right: train properly, think clearly, execute well, and still lose. You can be the better team and still go home.** **That's a brutal thing to internalize when you're young and believe, as young people do, that the universe is basically fair. It isn't. Sport tells you that early, and often, and without apology.**

What it gave me in exchange was harder to name. Something about functioning inside uncertainty. Something about staying useful when things are falling apart.

Those aren't lessons you learn from wins. The 2013 World Series was a win, and it was wonderful, but it taught me joy, not resilience. What taught me resilience was the soccer goal my parents missed, the basketball games I was outrun in, the tennis courts where I practiced my serves in front of no audience at all.

![0.8](/images/range.png)

*Me doing a corny pose with my dad at a shooting range, while holding the SIG MPX all wrong*

I'll close where I should have started: this is, in the end, a gratitude letter.

To my dad, who drove me to every practice session, sat through every mediocre performance, watched every final with the same attention whether it mattered or not. The man who switched allegiances to root for a baseball team from Boston because of something that happened at a marathon, and explained none of it to me until I was old enough to understand. Who spoke about Federer like he was teaching me something about character, not tennis. Who gave me a pass on IPL during exam season for one final, and was right to.

I don't think he set out to give me a philosophy. I think he just loved sports, and I was nearby, and it transferred.

The best things usually happen that way.

Love you dad.

_-Ritvik_

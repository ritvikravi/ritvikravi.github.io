Somewhere on a hard disk in our home, there is a version of me that doesn't know how lucky he is.

He's three or four years old, and he has a favorite parent, and it isn't his mother. His dad is funny and plays with him and doesn't ask him to finish vegetables. His mother is warm, absolutely — but she is also the one who insists on the vegetables. She is, in the honest language of desi households, slightly more the disciplinarian. And so the three-year-old makes his preferences known with the diplomatic subtlety of a three-year-old.

I think about that version of me sometimes. I think about how my mother, who had left a full life to raise this unreasonable little person, never once made him feel like a burden for not choosing herself.

![0.6](/images/amma-ny.png)

*New York, sometime in the late 2000s. She carried me through every unfamiliar space and made the weight look effortless.*

She lost her father when I was four. She lost her mother when I was twelve. My sister was born the same year she lost her last parent — which means she walked into motherhood for the second time carrying a grief she never really put down, and she did it anyway, with the same steadiness she brought to everything.

I did not understand this at twelve. I don't think I fully understood it until I started running into my own walls later in life. The bad grades, the project models that crashed, the specific despair of realizing that wanting an outcome very badly isn't a guarantee that you'll get it. And every single time I found myself spinning out, I noticed that underneath the anxiety, I still fundamentally believed things would eventually resolve.

That baseline optimism didn't come from nowhere. It came from watching my mother hold hers against far worse odds.

My father taught me to analyze a problem. When something goes wrong, I reach for frameworks, for options, for the best next move. That is him, completely. But the thing underneath the analysis — the quiet conviction that the analysis is worth doing because things will eventually be okay — that is her. I don't know how she kept it, given what she carried. I only know she passed it on.

## The Apartment Potlucks

When we lived in Arizona, I remember that the Indian community in our apartment complex held potlucks. Every family brought something. One lady always brought samosas. Another brought sandwiches. My mother brought her idli.

![0.8](/images/amma-lv.png)

*Las Vegas, 2017. Navigating an elaborate, age-appropriate road trip she meticulously mapped out for an eleven-year-old.*

Her idlis were invariably the first to finish. People would circle back to our table, cornering her to ask for the exact measurements and the secret recipe. She never gave it away. She told it to me once years later, but I was too distracted to write it down, and I suspect the exact formulation is lost to me now.

I think about those moments more than I probably should. Not because of the food, but because of who my mother was in those spaces. She was easy, happy, and entirely herself—a person with her own friends, her own laughter, and an identity that existed completely independent of being anyone's mother or wife. That version of her existed. It still does, in glimpses. But I understand now, with a maturity I completely lacked as a kid, just how much of that independent self she deliberately partitioned off to build a foundation for us.

## The Shifting Clock

But time has a brutal way of updating the variables.

For most of my youth, I subconsciously viewed my parents as fixed coordinates in the universe—permanently strong, unyielding, and entirely immune to the clock. You go away to college, you submerge yourself in work, and then you come back home for a break and the illusions start to drop.

You notice the micro-shifts. The woman who used to easily clock 10,000 steps a day during our family trips a few years ago now starts to huff a little during a casual 3,000-step evening walk around the neighborhood. My dad, a man I have literally never seen sit idle or admit to physical fatigue in my entire life, now occasionally has to lie down on the living room couch simply because he is tired.

It is a quiet, heavy kind of realization. It breaks your heart, but it also clears your vision. It forces you to realize that the window you have to execute your ambitions and show them the results isn't infinite. It strips away the luxury of procrastination. Every late night spent working on a project or optimizing a design isn't just about personal validation anymore—it’s a race against their timeline.

I don't know how you mathematically repay a structural sacrifice like the one she made. I'm not sure the ledger can ever truly be balanced. But I know exactly what I am building toward.

She deserves, at the absolute minimum, to see that idli recipe make the rounds again. This time, somewhere with a Manhattan skyline or a proper global landscape in the background. Somewhere she chose entirely for herself, where she doesn't have to be a disciplinarian, a manager, or a protector. Just herself.

That is the milestone. That has always been the milestone.

Happy birthday, Amma.

> **The Reality Check (TL;DR)**
> I co-organized a team of 6 verticals to pull off TEDxIITTirupati for over 100 attendees on a zero-profit budget. It involved navigating TEDx licensing constraints, handling a speaker nearly pulling out hours before the event, and crashing a rental scooter into a gravel patch at 70 km/h the afternoon before showtime.

On Apr 12, 2026, our team put on TEDxIITTirupati. It was the result of roughly four months of structured work, a license application that required more iteration than I expected, six functional verticals running in parallel, and the occasional external stakeholder who tested what "staying professional" really meant. I co-organized this entire event alongside Kevin, whose patience and clarity held the operation together, and a team that, at its best, functioned with a coherence I hadn't seen before in a student-run operation.


## Getting the License

The TEDx university license isn’t easy to get, and honestly, it shouldn’t be. There are a few important conditions: no sponsor or company branding behind the speakers, clean TEDx and university branding, and most importantly, a talk lineup that reflects more than a single worldview.

We learned that last part the hard way. Our initial application included three talk ideas. All three were entrepreneurship-oriented. TEDx's feedback was pretty direct: the talk ideas were too similar, not enough variety. They also made it clear that TEDx wasn’t meant for generic motivational speaking or neatly packaged success stories, but rather meant to surface local culture, local voices, and ideas that represent the university, the city, and the region.

That feedback reoriented how we thought about the event entirely. We revised, resubmitted, and got the license accepted, followed by a 20-30 minute debrief call with the people at the TEDx headquarters to walk through the plan.

## What leadership actually looked like

Before this project, my mental model of leadership was closer to enforcement. My assumption was simple: zero tolerance for mistakes produces efficiency.

That model fails completely on a voluntary team. If people fear the consequences, they don't work better; they just stop telling you when things are breaking and some disengage from working entirely.

Furthermore, our campus has a deeply entrenched age hierarchy, the standard "bhaiyya/anna" dynamic, which naturally suffocates upward feedback. Because I was a third-year student leading a team comprised mostly of first- and second-years, that distance was a serious liability. If a freshman was too intimidated to tell me that a technical delivery was failing, the whole event would take the hit.

To break that distance, I deliberately bypassed formal check-ins for casual, one-on-one interactions at the campus cafe. If I saw a team member there, I’d buy them something, sit down, and talk about anything other than the deliverables for TEDx. The point wasn't to be liked. I was working to build an environment where someone felt secure enough to bring a problem to me before it turned into an irreversible crisis.

## An accident right before the event

About 20 hours before showtime, we needed to pick up an arriving speaker from the airport. Lacking options, me and two of my team members did what any desperate student organizers would do: we tripled up on a single rental scooter and headed down the highway.

About 300 feet ahead of a major speed bump, moving at roughly 70 km/h, I pulled the brakes.

Nothing happened. The scooter’s brakes barely worked.

By the time we hit the 100-foot mark, we were still flying at 40 km/h, and the tarmac right before the bump was a graveyard of loose gravel. We hit the gravel and the scooter slid sideways immediately.

I took the brunt of the impact. My knees hit the asphalt first, followed by my shoulder and head. The brand-new, official TEDx t-shirt and cargo pants I was supposed to wear on stage the next morning were torn to shreds. I remember sitting on the roadside, trying to stay conscious through the shock, while the other two, bleeding from their elbows, dragged the scooter up. Realizing the clock was ticking, they checked that I was breathing, handed me a water bottle, and bolted for the airport. The speaker couldn't be left stranded.

The owners of a local *dhaba* right next to the crash site helped me stay conscious until they'd returned. They sat me upright, kept me conscious through a mounting headache, and poured water and turmeric over my raw skin.

That evening, I tracked down a local tailor in a frantic bid to save my ruined official gear. The stitching job she pulled off saved me. She asked for 60 INR; I gave her 100 because, at that moment, that thin black thread was the only thing keeping the visual presentation of the event intact.

## Conflict is inevitable; your response isn't

The physical shock of the crash was immediately followed by a test of emotional regulation. With mere hours before opening remarks, a speaker’s manager called and threatened to pull their client from the lineup entirely over a sudden dispute regarding car arrangements.

The manager had already been difficult throughout the week. Days of radio silence followed by aggressive, late-night calls demanding immediate deviations from our strict budget, including luxury accommodations we simply couldn't afford and hadn't promised.

Now, sitting in a room smelling of antiseptic, watching my co-organizers wrap bandages over open wounds, I was listening to someone scream at me through a phone speaker about premium vehicles.

I kept my voice entirely flat, refused to match the escalation, and calmly laid out the hard physical boundaries of our budget and what we could realistically execute. I ended up neutralizing the leverage they thought they had. The shouting stopped. The speaker showed up the next morning, performed exceptionally, and the schedule held.

## What it left me with

When the lights came up the next morning, our battered airport dispatch team was on-site. The other two had their elbows were covered in bandages, and I spent the entire event limping heavily on my right leg.

We kept our faces completely blank and ran the schedule. Very few got to know we had been sliding across asphalt twenty hours prior.

TEDx taught me that leadership is ultimately an exercise in internal persistence. Systems, timelines, spreadsheets, and budgets are essential structural components, but they are completely useless if the people running them lose faith in the operator.
*— Ritvik*

---
title: How I Became suspiciously good at Em Dashes
date: 2026-05-17
type: note
tag: Observations
summary: The unintended consequences of 'proper' punctuation in an age of LLM paranoia
---

There's a version of this story where I'm the villain. I'm going to try and convince you I'm not.

It starts in late 2023, when I, like most people with a laptop and a strong will to try the latest tech thing everyone was talking about, started using ChatGPT. Early ChatGPT had a very distinct personality. It was enthusiastic. It **loved** em dashes — those long, confident horizontal lines that appear in sentences like this one — as if it had a particular em dash quota to fill and was going to hit it no matter what.

At the time, I didn't fully understand the difference between a hyphen, an en dash, and an em dash. I had been using hyphens my entire life with the conviction of someone who had never been corrected. But then I noticed the em dashes immediately. They felt off. Way too formal, too deliberate — so I removed them from anything I drafted with AI assistance before sending it out. Early instinct. Good call.

Then the internet caught on fast. People started calling out the em dash as a "ChatGPT giveaway," right alongside all the unsolicited emojis, the bullet points that appeared when nobody asked for them, the "In conclusion" openers, and the very particular brand of symmetrical, logical phrasing — *"If A, then B; if not A, then consider C"* — that reads less like natural writing and more like a bot trying too hard to pass a logic test.

AI writing, at its most recognizable, sounds like someone who has read everything and experienced nothing.

Here's where I went wrong. Or right. I still haven't decided.

I actually looked up what em dashes were. Turns out: genuinely useful. A way to add emphasis, insert an aside, or break a sentence with precision — without the awkwardness of parentheses or the weakness of a comma. I started using them. Correctly. In my own writing. In text messages, even.

And that is when things went sideways.

I was in an argument. (The argument itself is not important. What's important is that I was clearly right.) Midway through making my point, I used an em dash. Naturally. Correctly. The other person — who, I should note with the most affectionate possible framing, treated punctuation as optional decoration and communicated largely in abbreviations, emojis, and a multilingual blend that linguists a hundred years from now would call "interesting" — stopped engaging with my actual point entirely.

"you copied that frm gpt??"

I had not.

"that was defo frm chatgpt."

It was not.

"your literally using ai to argue with me"

The em dash became the centerpiece of a trial I had not prepared for. The argument, whatever it was originally about, shifted entirely into a referendum on my punctuation choices. I lost the argument. Not on merit — on aesthetics.

The irony is almost perfect. I learned punctuation properly. I applied it naturally. And I became *more* suspicious than the people who never learned it at all. The reward for knowing the difference between an en dash and an em dash, in the modern times, is being accused of outsourcing your thoughts to a language model.

I don't resent AI for any of this. It did, technically, set the whole thing in motion. But the real lesson here is less about artificial intelligence and more about perception. In an era where AI writes like an overconfident student, knowing things *too well* starts to look like you didn't know them yourself.

I've thought about just going back to hyphens. Simpler. Safer. Statistically less likely to end an argument about whether I'm a human being.

But then again — and I say this with full awareness of how it sounds — I've worked too hard on this.

*- Ritvik*
